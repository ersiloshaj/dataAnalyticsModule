Visual Hierarchy & Dashboard Design

A dashboard is a decision-support tool. This page explains how to guide attention, reduce confusion, and design screens that tell a clear story - before you write a single line of code.

What “good” looks like

A stakeholder opens your dashboard and, within 5 seconds, they should know: (1) what they’re looking at, (2) what question it answers, and (3) what to click next.

Goal: make the correct interpretation the easiest interpretation. The design should “pull” the eye through the page in the right order.
Dashboards are not reports.
Reports are linear (page 1 → page 2). Dashboards are exploratory: users jump around, filter, compare, and revisit.
Design rule:
If everything is visually loud, nothing is important. Your job is to control emphasis.

Visual hierarchy toolkit

Hierarchy is how you control what gets noticed first, second, and third. You do it with a small set of levers:

1) Size

  • Bigger = more important. KPI numbers are usually largest.
  • Chart titles are bigger than axis labels. Filters are smaller than results.

2) Contrast

  • High contrast draws attention. Use it sparingly.
  • Mute “supporting” elements: gridlines, secondary labels, helper text.

3) Position

  • Top-left gets attention first (for left-to-right readers).
  • Put your primary story near the top, and details below.

4) Grouping

  • Things close together are perceived as related.
  • Use whitespace to separate sections; don’t rely on heavy borders everywhere.

5) Repetition

  • Repeat styles: same KPI cards, same chart header layout, same spacing rhythm.
  • Consistency reduces cognitive load.
Practical tip: design in “levels”. Level 1 = page title + headline KPI. Level 2 = 2-4 supporting visuals. Level 3 = details, notes, small tables.

Dashboard layout patterns

Most dashboards can be built using one of these simple patterns:

Pattern A: Filters left, results right

  • Great for teaching and first dashboards.
  • Works well for Shiny: sidebarPanel() + mainPanel().

Pattern B: Filters top, results below

  • Better on mobile and smaller screens.
  • Keeps horizontal space for charts.

Pattern C: Overview → Details

  • Top: one “anchor” chart showing the big picture.
  • Bottom: 2–3 supporting charts that explain drivers or breakdowns.
Avoid “everything everywhere” layouts.
If users must scroll endlessly, they lose context and stop comparing properly.

Choosing & shaping charts

Chart choice is part of hierarchy. Pick a chart that matches the question, then remove anything that doesn’t help answer it.

Match chart to question

  • Trend over time: line chart
  • Compare categories: bar chart (often sorted)
  • Distribution / variability: box plot
  • Relationship: scatter plot + trend
  • Part-to-whole: pie only when few categories (often better as bars)

Reduce chart noise

  • Use clear titles: what is plotted and what filters apply.
  • Lighten gridlines; avoid thick borders.
  • Label units (minutes, £, riders) and use sensible scales.
  • Prefer direct labels where possible (but don’t clutter).
Design habit: If a stakeholder screenshots one chart, does the image still make sense? Add enough context in the title/subtitle to stand alone.

Filters & interaction rules

Filters control “what universe the dashboard is talking about”. They should be obvious, predictable, and never feel like they break the page.

Rules of thumb

  • Put filters near the top (left panel or top row).
  • Default to sensible starting values (e.g., all cities, last 12 months).
  • Use one filtered dataset that drives all outputs (increased consistency and reduces load times).
  • Show empty states politely (“No rows match your filters” - leave no room for ambiguity).
Interaction anti-pattern:
If the user changes a filter and only one chart updates, they stop trusting the dashboard.

Interaction ladder

  • Level 1: Filter → everything updates
  • Level 2: Hover → tooltips
  • Level 3: Click → highlight or drill-down
  • Level 4: Brush/zoom → focus on a region

Titles, labels, and microcopy

Writing is part of design. A good title reduces the amount of time someone spends guessing.

Good titles are specific

  • Bad: “Ridership”
  • Better: “Average daily riders over time (selected cities & modes)”
  • Best: “Average daily riders over time - London & Bus - Jan 2023 to Dec 2024”

Use subtitles for assumptions

  • “Monthly totals; missing months excluded”
  • “Prices shown in £; mean ticket price per route-month”
  • “Delays in minutes; negative values removed”

Microcopy that prevents confusion

  • “Select multiple cities” (so users don’t assume one-only)
  • “Double-click to reset zoom” (if using Plotly)
  • “Hover points for details” (so they discover tooltips)
If users keep asking the same question, answer it in the UI.

Common mistakes

Everything is a KPI
Showing 12 big numbers makes none of them meaningful. Choose 3-5 that represent the dashboard’s purpose.
Too many chart types
Switching styles every panel makes comparison harder. Keep repeated patterns where possible.
Unsorted bars
If the goal is comparison, sort the bars (or use a meaningful order).
Titles that don’t describe filters
Users forget what they selected. Remind them in the subtitle.
Layout trap: cramming everything above the fold. It’s often better to show fewer panels with stronger hierarchy than many panels with weak hierarchy.

Trust-breakers

  • Different totals across charts due to inconsistent filtering
  • Axes that change scale unexpectedly when filters change
  • Numbers without units
  • Charts without context (“what is this measuring?”)

QA checklist

Use this when reviewing your dashboard before submitting.

  • Purpose: can you state the decision this dashboard supports in one sentence?
  • Hierarchy: do KPIs and the main chart get attention first?
  • Consistency: do all charts respond to the same filters?
  • Clarity: are titles specific and do they mention scope/filters?
  • Noise: have you reduced gridlines, clutter, and redundant legends?
  • Comparison: are bars sorted and units labelled?
  • Edge cases: do empty filters show a friendly message?
Idea: Ask a classmate to use your dashboard without instructions and watch where they get stuck.

Short exercises

Exercise 1: “10 second test”

Show your dashboard to a classmate for 10 seconds, then hide it. Ask them: “What is this dashboard about?” and “What would you click first?”

Exercise 2: Reduce one chart

Pick one plot and remove three things that don’t help interpretation (legend, heavy gridlines, too many categories, unclear title, etc.).

Exercise 3: Write better titles

Rewrite two titles so that they auto-update to include: metric, population/scope, and time window.

Exercise 4: Hierarchy map

On paper (or in a screenshot editor), circle the first, second, and third things your eye sees on your dashboard. If it’s not KPI → anchor chart → supporting detail, adjust size/contrast/position until it is.
